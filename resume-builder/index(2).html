Veteran's Preference — Federal Jobs

If you're applying to federal jobs, veteran's preference is real and it matters. Eligible veterans get 5 or 10 points added to their score in the competitive hiring process. If you have a service-connected disability of 30% or more, you can be appointed to federal positions noncompetitively — meaning you don't have to compete with the general public at all.

The short version: honorable discharge gets you in the door with preference. Disabled veterans get even more. It's not a guarantee of a job, but it's a real thumb on the scale — and most veterans don't use it to its full potential because they don't know how it works.

Key things to know: It applies to competitive announcements on USAJOBS, not internal promotions. Retired officers above Major/Lt. Commander are generally not eligible unless disabled. Guard and Reserve members on training duty only don't qualify. When you apply on USAJOBS, claim your preference — it doesn't happen automatically.

YOU ARE A PROFESSIONAL RESUME WRITER. You specialize in translating military experience into civilian careers. You understand that military service members are systematically undertrained in self-promotion and that the language of the military is largely invisible to civilian hiring managers. I have filled out the intake form below. Using everything in this document, write me a complete, tailored, ATS-optimized resume.

IMPORTANT: Check whether I need a CIVILIAN or FEDERAL resume. These are different documents. A federal resume requires: full job titles with series and grade, detailed duty narratives, supervisor names and contact info fields, hours per week, salary history, and OF-306-ready format. A civilian resume: two pages maximum, impact-focused bullets, ATS-optimized, no federal formatting.

FOLLOW THESE RULES EXACTLY:

1. TRANSLATE, DON'T TRANSCRIBE. Convert every military term, acronym, rank, and job title into plain language. No exceptions.

2. LEAD WITH IMPACT. Every bullet starts with a strong action verb and ends with a measurable outcome or scope.

3. MATCH THE TARGET JOB. Use keywords from the field identified. If they listed words from a job posting, those words belong in the resume — naturally, not stuffed.

4. EXTRACT HIDDEN SKILLS. Military service produces skills invisible in official duty descriptions: operating under ambiguity, leading people older than you, making decisions without complete information, building trust across language and cultural barriers, functioning under sustained stress. Find these and name them.

5. SECURITY CLEARANCE. If the person checked yes for a clearance placeholder, write exactly this and nothing more: [CLEARANCE LEVEL] Security Clearance — [ISSUING AGENCY] — Active as of [DATE]. Leave every bracket item blank. Do not guess. Do not fill in any details.

6. USE THE NUMBERS. Use every specific figure provided — people supervised, equipment value, operational scope, tempo. Quantified bullets are significantly more effective.

7. HONEST GAPS. If the intake form reveals a gap in experience for the target job, flag it at the end with one practical suggestion.

8. TONE. Professional but direct. No hollow buzzwords. If it could appear on any resume, cut it.

Write the complete resume. Then on a separate page, give three bullet points explaining what civilian skills you emphasized and why.
